Overview

Overview - Unbalanced Wheel

In this lab, you will analyze the motion of a wheel rolling down a ramp in two configurations: balanced and unbalanced. The unbalanced configuration includes an eccentric mass, which changes the wheel's rotational behavior.

Your job is to compare measured angular velocity from encoder data against a provided model hierarchy. The focus is not on inventing the dynamics model from scratch. Instead, you will review the provided unverified derivation, implement the model carefully, process the data, and evaluate how well each model explains the observed motion.

By the end of the lab, you should be able to:

  • convert encoder counts and timestamps into angular position and angular velocity
  • compare balanced and unbalanced wheel behavior
  • estimate or apply a friction term from balanced-wheel data
  • compute residuals between measurements and model predictions
  • explain whether model mismatch appears random, systematic, or tied to known apparatus limits

The sample data for this lab is provided through SharePoint rather than bundled into the course site.

Procedure — Unbalanced Wheel: Inertia Distribution and Rolling Dynamics

Overview

This lab investigates the dynamics of a cylindrical wheel rolling down a ramp under two configurations: balanced (no eccentric mass) and unbalanced (eccentric mass installed). Students analyze encoder data and compare it against a provided four-model hierarchy.

The lab has two distinct parts that must be done in sequence:

  1. Pre-Lab (before the lab session): Review and verify the provided model derivation, then implement the MATLAB function. This must be complete before you handle the apparatus.
  2. In-Lab: Run the apparatus, collect data, and perform analysis.

Before arriving at the apparatus:

  • Record any correction or unresolved concern in your analysis notes

Unverified Derivation

Provided Unverified Derivation — Unbalanced Wheel: Inertia Distribution and Rolling Dynamics

How Students Should Use This Derivation

This derivation is provided so the lab can focus on experimental data analysis, model implementation, and model-data comparison rather than on dynamics derivation from a blank page. It is not externally verified.

Students should verify the derivation before using it as the basis for their analysis. Verification means checking the assumptions, signs, units, limiting cases, and energy terms well enough to decide whether the model is appropriate for comparison with the measured data.

Recommended verification checks:

  • Confirm the rolling-without-slip constraint: v_cm = R*omega
  • Confirm the ramp-height relation: h = R*theta*sin(beta)
  • Confirm that all kinetic-energy terms have units of joules
  • Confirm that the friction moment contributes negative work: -Q*theta
  • Check the unbalanced-mass potential term by evaluating theta = 0
  • Check the difference between the point-mass model and rigid-body model
  • Check whether the provided rigid-body inertia term is being counted once,

not double-counted with translational kinetic energy

The goal is not to re-create the derivation from scratch. The goal is to be able to defend the model you use when you compare it with data.

Section 1 — Physical Setup and Coordinate System

A cylindrical wheel (mass M, radius R, radius of gyration κ) rolls down a ramp (slope angle β) without slipping. A trailing support apparatus (mass M₀) is rigidly attached to the wheel; it translates but does not rotate. An eccentric mass (mass m) can be bolted to the wheel at radius r from the wheel center.

Coordinate system:

  • θ = rotation angle, measured clockwise from the perpendicular vector into the ramp
  • θ = 0 defined as the position when the wheel starts moving from rest
  • For unbalanced trials: eccentric mass is positioned perpendicular to the ramp surface at θ = 0

Rolling constraint (no-slip): v_cm = R · ω

where v_cm is the velocity of the wheel center and ω = dθ/dt.

Section 2 — Work-Energy Principle (General Form)

The work-energy principle applied to the complete system:

W_net = ΔKE = KE_final − KE_initial

Starting from rest: KE_initial = 0 for all trials (θ = 0, ω = 0 at start).

The kinetic energy of the rolling system includes:

  • Rotational KE of the wheel: (1/2) · I · ω²
  • Translational KE of the wheel center: (1/2) · M · v_cm²
  • Translational KE of the trailing apparatus: (1/2) · M₀ · v_cm²

Using the rolling constraint v_cm = R · ω, all KE terms can be expressed as functions of ω.

For the unbalanced configuration, the eccentric mass adds both translational and rotational KE (the latter only in MODEL_4).

Work done by gravity = (total mass) · g · (vertical displacement of center of mass) Work done by friction moment = −Q · θ (where Q is the constant negative moment)

Section 3 — MODEL_1: Balanced Wheel, No Friction

Configuration: Wheel and trailing apparatus only; no eccentric mass; no friction moment.

Assumptions:

  • Cylinder rolls without slipping
  • Trailing apparatus translates but does not rotate
  • No friction at the axle

Using work-energy:


(M + M0)*g*R*theta*sin(beta) = 0.5*((M + M0)*R^2 + M*kappa^2)*omega^2

Solving for angular velocity:


omega_1(theta) = sqrt(
 2*(M + M0)*g*R*theta*sin(beta)
 / ((M + M0)*R^2 + M*kappa^2)
)

Parameters used: M, M₀, R, κ, β, g

Section 4 — MODEL_2: Balanced Wheel, Constant Friction Moment

Modification of MODEL_1: Add a constant negative moment −Q applied to the shaft of the wheel. Q represents friction at the axle; its value is unknown and must be determined empirically.

Assumptions: Same as MODEL_1 plus constant negative moment −Q.

The friction moment does negative work:


W_Q = -Q*theta

The energy equation becomes:


(M + M0)*g*R*theta*sin(beta) - Q*theta
 = 0.5*((M + M0)*R^2 + M*kappa^2)*omega^2

Solving for angular velocity:


omega_2(theta, Q) = sqrt(
 2*((M + M0)*g*R*theta*sin(beta) - Q*theta)
 / ((M + M0)*R^2 + M*kappa^2)
)

Empirical determination of Q:

  • Q is not known from first principles; it must be fit to balanced experimental data
  • Method: try 5 or more values of Q; identify which value best matches the experimental ω(θ) curve for both balanced trials
  • The same value of Q must work for both balanced trials
  • Q determined here is then carried forward into MODEL_3 and MODEL_4 unchanged

Parameters used: M, M₀, R, κ, β, g, Q (empirically determined)

Section 5 — MODEL_3: Unbalanced Wheel, Eccentric Mass as Particle

Modification of MODEL_2: Add the eccentric mass m at radius r, modeled as a point particle (all mass concentrated at a point — no rotational inertia about its own center).

Assumptions: Same as MODEL_2 plus eccentric mass modeled as particle at radius r.

The eccentric mass center moves in a circle of radius r about the wheel center. Its velocity has two components that depend on both ω and the current angular position θ.

The extra mass changes height both because the wheel center moves down the ramp and because the mass rotates around the wheel center. Relative to the initial configuration:


Delta_h_extra = R*theta*sin(beta) - r*cos(theta + beta) + r*cos(beta)

The translational speed of the eccentric mass contributes the factor:


v_extra^2 = omega^2*(R^2 + 2*R*r*cos(theta) + r^2)

Combining the balanced-wheel energy, the eccentric-mass potential-energy change, the eccentric-mass translational kinetic energy, and the friction work:


omega_3(theta, Q) = sqrt(
 2*((M + M0)*g*R*theta*sin(beta)
 + m*g*(R*theta*sin(beta) - r*cos(theta + beta) + r*cos(beta))
 - Q*theta)
 / ((M + M0)*R^2 + M*kappa^2 + m*(R^2 + 2*R*r*cos(theta) + r^2))
)

Parameters used: M, M₀, m, R, r, κ, β, g, Q (from MODEL_2)

Section 6 — MODEL_4: Unbalanced Wheel, Eccentric Mass as Rigid Body

Modification of MODEL_3: Model the eccentric mass as a rigid body (solid cylinder/rod) rather than a point particle. This adds a rotational kinetic energy term for the eccentric mass rotating about its own center of mass.

Moment of inertia of the eccentric mass about its own center: I_extra = (1/2) · m · r_extra² where r_extra = 0.019 m

The rigid-body version uses the same numerator as MODEL_3. The difference is the additional rotational kinetic energy of the eccentric mass about its own center:


I_extra = 0.5*m*r_extra^2


omega_4(theta, Q) = sqrt(
 2*((M + M0)*g*R*theta*sin(beta)
 + m*g*(R*theta*sin(beta) - r*cos(theta + beta) + r*cos(beta))
 - Q*theta)
 / ((M + M0)*R^2 + M*kappa^2
 + m*(R^2 + 2*R*r*cos(theta) + r^2)
 + I_extra)
)

Verification note: the sample report describes the rigid-body model using the parallel-axis theorem. Students should check whether the orbital contribution m*r^2 is already included in v_extra^2 before adding any additional parallel-axis term.

Parameters used: M, M₀, m, R, r, r_extra, κ, β, g, Q (from MODEL_2)

Section 7 — Residual Analysis Framework

After fitting models to data, quantify the agreement using residual statistics.

Residual definition: ε_i = ω_observed,i − ω_predicted(θ_i)

Important: Do not take the absolute value of residuals. A non-zero mean residual indicates systematic bias in the model.

Evaluation rule: Evaluate the model at the experimentally measured θ values — not at a uniform grid. The residuals must be computed at the same θ_i values where ω_observed,i was measured.

Statistics to compute:

StatisticFormulaInterpretation
Mean residualmean(ε) = (1/N) Σ ε_iNon-zero → systematic bias
Standard deviationσ(ε) = std(ε)Magnitude of random error
Uncertainty of meanσ_mean = σ / √NConfidence in mean estimate
Ncount of observationsNumber of data points in reliable range
Outlier countcount(ε_i> 3σ)Number of residuals beyond 3σ

Table to produce: One row per model per trial; columns: σ, mean(ε), σ/√N, N, count(|ε_i| > 3σ)

Histogram: Plot histogram of residuals scaled by σ (i.e., ε_i / σ); superimpose a standard normal distribution N(0,1) for comparison. This reveals whether errors are normally distributed.

  • Meriam, J.L. & Kraige, L.G. Engineering Mechanics: Dynamics. (Work-energy methods for rigid bodies)

FAQs

FAQs - Unbalanced Wheel

Do I need to derive the model from scratch?

No. You are given a provided unverified derivation. You should review and verify the reasoning, signs, assumptions, and units before using it, but the main lab task is model implementation and comparison against data.

Which encoder channel should I use?

If both QuadEncoder and LS7184Encoder appear in the exported CSV, compare the channels and justify which one you use for final angle and angular velocity calculations.

Why should I trim the data range?

The recommended reliable range is approximately 0.5 rad < theta < 15 rad. Very early data may include startup effects, and very late data may include end-of-ramp or stopping effects.

What residual should I compute?

Use a signed residual and state your convention. A common choice is:


residual = omega_observed - omega_predicted

Why are the raw data files not on the site?

Large or frequently updated data files are distributed through SharePoint. Use the student-facing data folder linked in the Sample Data section.

What should I discuss if the model does not match perfectly?

Discuss likely physical and measurement causes: encoder noise, wireless packet drops, release variability, start/end transients, friction modeling, and whether the eccentric mass is better treated as a point mass or rigid body.
