Purpose and sequence

Then use the released Clark Y-14 pressure data, estimate uncertainty in the aerodynamic coefficients, and interpret the results against the supplied reference.

The required sequence is:

Airspeed measurement uncertainty → favorable-condition selection → airfoil pressure measurements → aerodynamic coefficients → coefficient uncertainty and model/data interpretation.

The tunnel maximum is 60 m/s. This is the tunnel's operating capability, not the range of every sensor. Work only within approved test directions and released measurement limits.

A. Measurement system and airspeed model

A Pitot-static system measures dynamic pressure, the difference between stagnation pressure and freestream static pressure:

$$ q=p_0-p_\infty. $$

Here, $q$ is dynamic pressure in pascals (Pa), $p_0$ is stagnation pressure in Pa, and $p_\infty$ is freestream static pressure in Pa. Atmospheric pressure $p$ and absolute temperature $T$ determine low-speed air density $\rho$:

$$ \rho=\frac{p}{R_{air}T},\qquad V=\sqrt{\frac{2q}{\rho}}=\sqrt{\frac{2qR_{air}T}{p}},\qquad R_{air}=287.05\;\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}. $$

In these equations, $p$ is absolute atmospheric pressure in Pa, $T$ is in kelvins (K), $\rho$ is density in $kg/m^3$, $V$ is derived freestream speed in $m/s$, and $R_{air}$ is the specific gas constant for air. For this low-speed model, atmospheric pressure $p$ is the freestream static pressure $p_\infty$.

Use measured $q,p,T$ once as the primitive airspeed inputs. Logged airspeed and density calculated from these inputs are derived values, not additional independent measurements. The Scanivalve measures airfoil surface pressure; it does not enter this Pitot-static airspeed model.

The DSA3217 is confirmed in the ±10 inH₂O configuration. Keep this scanner range distinct from the tunnel's confirmed 60 m/s maximum and the candidate Pitot sensor range. Use the numerical values in the sensor and data-input summary as the designated provisional calculation scenario. The specific installed Pitot, atmospheric-pressure, and temperature sensor identities/conversion paths remain provisional; they are not verified calibration facts. Do not search instructor-only solution files for specifications.

B. Airspeed uncertainty and condition selection

For independent primitive inputs, the first-order Taylor sensitivities of the speed model are:

$$ \frac{\partial V}{\partial q}=\frac{V}{2q},\qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial p}=-\frac{V}{2p},\qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial T}=\frac{V}{2T}. $$

Each sensitivity describes the local change in calculated speed per unit change in one input. For example, $(\partial V/\partial q)u_q$ is the speed-uncertainty contribution from dynamic pressure. For independent inputs, combine their contributions by root-sum-square:

$$ u_V=\sqrt{\left(\frac{V}{2q}u_q\right)^2+ \left(\frac{V}{2p}u_p\right)^2+ \left(\frac{V}{2T}u_T\right)^2},\qquad \frac{u_V}{V}=\frac12\sqrt{\left(\frac{u_q}{q}\right)^2+ \left(\frac{u_p}{p}\right)^2+ \left(\frac{u_T}{T}\right)^2}. $$

Here, $u_q$ and $u_p$ are standard uncertainties in Pa, $u_T$ is in K, and $u_V$ is in m/s. Absolute uncertainty describes expected speed uncertainty in m/s; normalized uncertainty is dimensionless and is often reported as a percentage. The normalized contribution from input $i$ is its individual Taylor contribution divided by $V$. The Taylor expressions for $u_V$ and $u_V/V$ are undefined at exactly $V=0$; do not evaluate them there.

Treat each released plus/minus bound $a$ as a rectangular distribution from $-a$ to $+a$, so the standard uncertainty is $u=a/\sqrt{3}$. Independence is a modeling assumption, not a demonstrated property of the installed instruments. Use the instructor-designated temperature case if more than one candidate bound is listed.

For the full 0–60 m/s tunnel envelope, make these plots:

  1. Combined normalized standard uncertainty $100u_V/V$ in percent versus speed. Plot relative uncertainty only at positive speeds.
  2. Absolute standard uncertainty $u_V$ in m/s versus speed, evaluated only where the Taylor model is defined.
  3. Individual normalized contributions $100u_{V,q}/V$, $100u_{V,p}/V$, and $100u_{V,T}/V$, together with the combined curve.

Explain the shapes and trends. Compare what the absolute and normalized plots say, identify dominant inputs, and distinguish the tunnel envelope from sensor-valid conditions. Show the sensor-range boundary and mark Taylor or other mathematical extrapolation beyond it. A result calculated outside an instrument's usable range is not a claim about achievable measurement accuracy. Check surface-pressure range separately when evaluating the airfoil data.

Use the plots and practical constraints to recommend favorable and unfavorable or unsupported regions. State your decision rule before applying it. A 3% threshold may be shown as an example only; it is not a universal engineering rule. Also consider whether the measurement signal and aerodynamic response would be useful.

C. Airfoil pressure measurements and force derivation

Use the approved student-accessible historical measurement file and data dictionary. The instructional-team source file is ASEN2802_InfiniteWing_FullRange (1).csv: 640 measured rows, 20 samples at each integer angle from −15° through +16°, at approximately 30.37 m/s. This is a historical single-speed sweep, not a newly measured multi-speed dataset.

The source file's 30 fields are: atmospheric temperature (K), atmospheric pressure (Pa), atmospheric density (kg/m³), logged airspeed (m/s), Pitot dynamic pressure (Pa), auxiliary dynamic pressure (Pa), actual voltage (V), angle of attack (deg), spanwise position (in), sting axial force (N), sting normal force (N), sting pitching moment (N·m), ELD probe x/y positions (mm), and Scanivalve pressure channels 1–16 (Pa). Use only columns designated by the approved dictionary. The reduction uses Pitot $q$ and surface pressures; auxiliary pressure and implausible sting loads are not used.

Pressure pushes normal to a surface. To find the aerodynamic force per unit span on a two-dimensional airfoil, add the pressure-force contributions around the complete closed contour. For each straight segment, the endpoint pressures are averaged to approximate the pressure along that segment. The sign of each contribution depends on the contour direction and the direction of the pressure difference across the surface.

Use $x$ positive from leading edge to trailing edge and $y$ positive upward. Traverse the contour clockwise: along the upper surface from leading edge to trailing edge, then along the lower surface back toward the leading edge, and finally across the leading-edge closing segment. The airfoil interior stays to the right of the direction of travel. Define axial force as positive in $+x$ and normal force as positive in $+y$.

For directed element $i$ from point $i$ to point $i+1$, let $\Delta x_i=x_{i+1}-x_i$, $\Delta y_i=y_{i+1}-y_i$, and $\Delta s_i$ be its length. Let $\theta_i$ be the signed angle measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$ axis to the segment. Then

$$ \cos\theta_i=\frac{\Delta x_i}{\Delta s_i},\qquad \sin\theta_i=\frac{\Delta y_i}{\Delta s_i}. $$

For this clockwise contour, the inward unit normal is the right-hand normal to the tangent:

$$ \hat{\mathbf n}_{in,i}=\sin\theta_i\,\hat{\mathbf x}-\cos\theta_i\,\hat{\mathbf y}. $$

Let $p_i$ be local surface pressure, $p_\infty$ freestream static pressure, and $\Delta p_i=p_i-p_\infty$. The average pressure difference on an element is $\overline{\Delta p}_i=(\Delta p_i+\Delta p_{i+1})/2$. Pressure force per unit span equals pressure difference times element length and points inward:

$$ \Delta\mathbf F'_{p,i}=\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta s_i\,\hat{\mathbf n}_{in,i}. $$

Resolve this elemental force into axial and normal components. Using $\Delta s_i\sin\theta_i=\Delta y_i$ and $\Delta s_i\cos\theta_i=\Delta x_i$ gives

$$ \Delta F'_{a,i}=\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta y_i,\qquad \Delta F'_{n,i}=-\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta x_i. $$

The length and angle cancel, so the implementation needs signed coordinate changes rather than a separately calculated angle. A prime denotes force per unit span; each elemental term has units Pa·m = N/m when SI units are used.

Sum all elements around the closed contour before forming coefficients:

$$ F'_a=\sum_i\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta y_i,\qquad F'_n=-\sum_i\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta x_i. $$

Completing the contour matters. On a closed contour, $\sum_i\Delta x_i=0$ and $\sum_i\Delta y_i=0$, so a constant freestream pressure contributes no net force. Thus the measured surface-to-freestream differences $\Delta p_i$ can be used directly. Disconnected ports are not measurements: do not invent values for them or include them as tap locations.

Freestream dynamic pressure is $q_\infty=\tfrac12\rho_\infty V_\infty^2$. Define pressure coefficient at point $i$ as

$$ C_{p,i}=\frac{p_i-p_\infty}{q_\infty}=\frac{\Delta p_i}{q_\infty}. $$

The sectional normal and axial coefficients are force per unit span divided by $q_\infty c$, where chord $c$ is in the same length units as the coordinates:

$$ C_n=\frac{F'_n}{q_\infty c},\qquad C_a=\frac{F'_a}{q_\infty c}. $$

The denominator has units Pa·m = N/m, so these coefficients are dimensionless. Substituting the force sums and applying the trapezoidal rule gives the discrete contour equations:

$$ C_n=-\sum_i\frac{C_{p,i}+C_{p,i+1}}{2}\frac{\Delta x_i}{c},\qquad C_a=+\sum_i\frac{C_{p,i}+C_{p,i+1}}{2}\frac{\Delta y_i}{c}. $$

For closely spaced elements, these sums approach the corresponding contour integrals:

$$ C_n=-\oint C_p\,\frac{dx}{c},\qquad C_a=+\oint C_p\,\frac{dy}{c}. $$

These are two-dimensional sectional coefficients based on force per unit span, not finite-wing planform lift or drag coefficients.

Finally, rotate the chord-axis force components into lift and drag axes referenced to the freestream. Let $\alpha$ be angle of attack, the angle between the chord reference and freestream direction using the course data's sign convention:

$$ C_l=C_n\cos\alpha-C_a\sin\alpha,\qquad C_d=C_n\sin\alpha+C_a\cos\alpha. $$

The normal-force component projects mostly into lift; its drag projection grows with angle of attack. The axial-force component projects mostly into drag and subtracts from lift for positive $\alpha$ under this convention. MATLAB's sin and cos expect radians; convert measured degrees with deg2rad, or use sind and cosd.

The equations below follow the pressure-integration method used in the instructor implementation. The sensor scenario supports the calculation; it does not verify installed hardware identities.

1. Measurement system and airspeed model

$$ q=p_0-p_\infty. $$

Here, $q$ is dynamic pressure in pascals (Pa), $p_0$ is stagnation pressure in Pa, and $p_\infty$ is freestream static pressure in Pa. Atmospheric pressure $p$ and absolute temperature $T$ determine low-speed air density $\rho$:

In these equations, $p$ is absolute atmospheric pressure in Pa, $T$ is absolute temperature in kelvins (K), $\rho$ is density in kg/m$^3$, $V$ is derived freestream speed in m/s, and $R_{air}$ is the specific gas constant for air. In this low-speed model, the atmospheric pressure $p$ is the freestream static pressure $p_\infty$.

Use measured $q,p,T$ once as the primitive airspeed inputs. Logged airspeed and density calculated from these inputs are derived values, not additional independent measurements. The Scanivalve measures airfoil surface pressure; it does not enter this Pitot-static airspeed model.

2. Airspeed uncertainty propagation

For independent primitive inputs, the first-order Taylor sensitivities are:

$$ \frac{\partial V}{\partial q}=\frac{V}{2q},\qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial p}=-\frac{V}{2p},\qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial T}=\frac{V}{2T}. $$

Each sensitivity says how much the calculated speed changes per unit change in one input near its nominal value. For example, $(\partial V/\partial q)u_q$ is the speed uncertainty contribution from dynamic pressure. The sensitivities have units of (m/s)/Pa for $q$ and $p$, and (m/s)/K for $T$. Combining independent contributions by root-sum-square gives the absolute standard uncertainty:

$$ u_V= \sqrt{ \left(\frac{V}{2q}u_q\right)^2+ \left(\frac{V}{2p}u_p\right)^2+ \left(\frac{V}{2T}u_T\right)^2 }. $$

Dividing by nominal speed gives normalized standard uncertainty:

$$ \frac{u_V}{V} =\frac{1}{2} \sqrt{ \left(\frac{u_q}{q}\right)^2+ \left(\frac{u_p}{p}\right)^2+ \left(\frac{u_T}{T}\right)^2 }. $$

Here $u_q$ and $u_p$ are standard uncertainties in Pa, $u_T$ is in K, and $u_V$ is in m/s. Absolute uncertainty describes the expected speed error in m/s; normalized uncertainty is dimensionless and is often reported as a percentage. These expressions are not evaluated at exactly $V=0$.

For the designated calculation scenario, treat each stated plus/minus bound $a$ as an independent rectangular distribution from $-a$ to $+a$. Its standard uncertainty is $u=a/\sqrt{3}$. Independence is a model assumption, not a demonstrated property of the installed instruments. The DSA3217 surface-pressure scanner is separate from the $q,p,T$ airspeed chain. The confirmed tunnel maximum is 60 m/s; the confirmed scanner configuration is ±10 inH$_2$O. Candidate Pitot, atmospheric-pressure, and temperature sensor identities remain provisional.

3. Pressure forces on a two-dimensional airfoil

Pressure pushes normal to a surface. To obtain the aerodynamic force per unit span on a two-dimensional airfoil, add the pressure-force contributions over its complete closed contour. The sign of a contribution depends on the surface direction and the pressure difference across it.

Use chordwise coordinate $x$ positive from leading edge to trailing edge and thickness coordinate $y$ positive upward. Traverse the contour clockwise: along the upper surface from leading edge to trailing edge, then along the lower surface back toward the leading edge, and finally across the small leading-edge closing segment. The airfoil interior stays to the right of the direction of travel. Define axial force as positive in the $+x$ direction and normal force as positive in the $+y$ direction.

For tap or contour point $i$:

  • $p_i$ is the local airfoil surface pressure in Pa.
  • $p_\infty$ is freestream static pressure in Pa.
  • $\Delta p_i=p_i-p_\infty$ is the local pressure difference in Pa.
  • $(x_i,y_i)$ is the point coordinate. Coordinates may be in inches or metres, but use one consistent unit system within each calculation.
  • $c$ is airfoil chord length in the same length units as the coordinates.
  • $\Delta s_i$ is the length of the straight surface element from point $i$ to point $i+1$.
  • $\Delta x_i=x_{i+1}-x_i$ and $\Delta y_i=y_{i+1}-y_i$ are signed coordinate changes.
  • $\theta_i$ is the signed angle measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$ axis to the directed segment from point $i$ to point $i+1$.
  • $F_a'$ and $F_n'$ are axial and normal forces per unit span, respectively, in N/m.

The average pressure difference on element $i$ is $\overline{\Delta p}_i=(\Delta p_i+\Delta p_{i+1})/2$.

4. Elemental force derivation

The tangent segment follows the contour from point $i$ to point $i+1$. With the angle convention above:

$$ \cos\theta_i=\frac{\Delta x_i}{\Delta s_i},\qquad \sin\theta_i=\frac{\Delta y_i}{\Delta s_i}. $$

Because the contour is clockwise, the inward unit normal is the right-hand normal to the tangent:

$$ \hat{\mathbf n}_{in,i} =\sin\theta_i\,\hat{\mathbf x} -\cos\theta_i\,\hat{\mathbf y}. $$

For a straight element, approximate the pressure difference as its endpoint average. Pressure force per unit span equals pressure difference times element length, directed inward:

$$ \Delta\mathbf F'_{p,i} =\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta s_i\,\hat{\mathbf n}_{in,i}. $$

Project onto the positive axial and normal axes:

$$ \Delta F'_{a,i} =\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta s_i\sin\theta_i =\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta y_i, $$

$$ \Delta F'_{n,i} =-\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta s_i\cos\theta_i =-\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta x_i. $$

The segment length and angle cancel because $\Delta s_i\sin\theta_i=\Delta y_i$ and $\Delta s_i\cos\theta_i=\Delta x_i$. The implementation therefore needs the endpoint coordinates, not a separately calculated segment angle. Each force contribution has units Pa·m = N/m.

5. Sum forces and nondimensionalize

Sum the elemental contributions around the entire closed contour before forming coefficients:

$$ F'_a=\sum_i\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta y_i,\qquad F'_n=-\sum_i\overline{\Delta p}_i\,\Delta x_i. $$

Completing the contour matters. A constant freestream pressure acting around a closed contour contributes no net force because the signed coordinate changes sum to zero:

$$ \sum_i\Delta x_i=0,\qquad \sum_i\Delta y_i=0. $$

Thus the constant $p_\infty$ terms cancel, and the measured surface-to-freestream differences $\Delta p_i$ can be used. Disconnected ports are not measurements: do not invent values for them or include them as tap locations.

The freestream dynamic pressure is

$$ q_\infty=\frac{1}{2}\rho_\infty V_\infty^2, $$

where $\rho_\infty$ is freestream density in kg/m$^3$ and $V_\infty$ is freestream speed in m/s. Define the pressure coefficient at point $i$ as

$$ C_{p,i}=\frac{p_i-p_\infty}{q_\infty} =\frac{\Delta p_i}{q_\infty}. $$

The sectional normal and axial coefficients are force per unit span divided by $q_\infty c$:

$$ C_n=\frac{F'_n}{q_\infty c},\qquad C_a=\frac{F'_a}{q_\infty c}. $$

This denominator has units Pa·m = N/m, so the coefficients are dimensionless. Substituting the force sums and using $\overline{C}_{p,i}=(C_{p,i}+C_{p,i+1})/2$ gives the discrete trapezoidal-rule equations:

$$ C_n=-\sum_i\frac{C_{p,i}+C_{p,i+1}}{2}\frac{\Delta x_i}{c}, $$

$$ C_a=+\sum_i\frac{C_{p,i}+C_{p,i+1}}{2}\frac{\Delta y_i}{c}. $$

In the limit of closely spaced elements, these sums are the corresponding closed-contour integrals:

$$ C_n=-\oint C_p\,\frac{dx}{c},\qquad C_a=+\oint C_p\,\frac{dy}{c}. $$

These are two-dimensional sectional coefficients based on force per unit span. They are not finite-wing planform lift or drag coefficients.

6. Rotate normal and axial into lift and drag

$C_n$ and $C_a$ are referenced to the airfoil chord axes. Lift and drag are referenced to the freestream axes. Let $\alpha$ be angle of attack: the angle between the chord reference and freestream direction, using the course data's sign convention. Project the chord-axis force components onto the freestream lift and drag axes:

$$ C_l=C_n\cos\alpha-C_a\sin\alpha, $$

$$ C_d=C_n\sin\alpha+C_a\cos\alpha. $$

The normal-force contribution projects mostly into lift, while its projection into drag grows with angle of attack. The axial-force contribution projects mostly into drag and subtracts from lift for positive $\alpha$ under this convention. MATLAB's sin and cos functions expect radians; convert measured degrees with deg2rad, or use the degree-specific sind and cosd functions.

Convert the measurements to pressure coefficients

For each selected angle, calculate:

$$ C_{p,i}=\frac{\Delta p_i}{q_\infty}. $$

Here, $\Delta p_i$ is already the surface-to-freestream pressure difference. Do not subtract atmospheric pressure from these columns a second time. Use the measured Pitot dynamic pressure as $q_\infty$ for the corresponding angle group.

8. Coefficient uncertainty by Monte Carlo

For every measured angle of attack included in the final coefficient plots, use 10,000 trials and the designated provisional rectangular bounds. These bounds are propagation assumptions, not confirmed calibration values. For each trial:

  1. Add one uniform perturbation from -49.8 Pa to +49.8 Pa to the measured Pitot dynamic pressure. Share this same perturbed $q$ across all 16 taps.
  2. Independently add a uniform perturbation from -4.9818 Pa to +4.9818 Pa to each measured surface-pressure channel.
  3. Add one uniform perturbation from -0.5° to +0.5° to the measured angle of attack.
  4. Recalculate the pressure coefficients, trailing-edge closure, full-contour $C_n$ and $C_a$, and rotated $C_l$ and $C_d$.
  5. Repeat for all 10,000 trials at that angle. Calculate the sample standard deviations $s_{C_l}$ and $s_{C_d}$.

Plot the sample standard deviations as ±1 combined-standard-uncertainty bars centered on the nominal measured coefficients. They are standard uncertainty bars, not 95% intervals. Independent channel errors are the provisional baseline because channel covariance has not been measured. Do not perturb atmospheric pressure, temperature, density, or derived airspeed again: the coefficient calculation uses measured $q$ as its denominator.

10. Reference comparison and limitations

Compare measured sectional $C_l(\alpha)$ and $C_d(\alpha)$ with the supplied Clark Y-14 reference data over its stated validity range. Evaluate agreement and disagreement; do not assume that agreement is required. Consider Reynolds-number and other condition differences, and distinguish coefficient uncertainty from model/reference discrepancy. The current historical pressure dataset is a single-speed sweep near 30.37 m/s, while the empirical NACA TR 628 reference is near $Re\approx3.02\times10^6$ and the measured sweep is near $Re\approx1.46\times10^5$. The comparison is limited to -8° through +12°. These data do not establish an aerodynamic response at other speeds or experimentally verify a recommended operating region.

Finite-wing planform coefficients, structural deflection, beam or spar calculations, and whiffle-tree analysis are outside this student task.

Additional Notes - Aeroelasticity Tier 2

Pedagogical purpose

The branch uses uncertainty analysis to select tunnel conditions before applying those conditions to an infinite-wing pressure survey. The main learning move is from pointwise pressure uncertainty to a condition-dependent recommendation about measured airspeed or dynamic pressure and angle of attack.

Recommended emphasis

  • Treat the measured velocity and angle of attack as test conditions, not merely metadata.
  • Use the pressure measurement chain to explain why the uncertainty changes across the test matrix.
  • Use the infinite-wing result as the physics-based comparison.
  • Keep sensor-input uncertainty, model-form discrepancy, and commanded-versus-measured

conditions separate.

  • Require a stated decision rule for appropriate, marginal, and uncertainty-dominated

conditions.

  • Require both scaled output uncertainty and normalized uncertainty contributions.

Four-day planning note

Day 1 should be measurement-chain modeling, uncertainty-based condition selection, and prediction. Day 2 should be the infinite-wing pressure survey at selected conditions. Day 3 should be coefficient reduction, uncertainty sweeps, and residual analysis. Day 4 should be targeted validation and the client recommendation.

Instructor decisions still required

  • Which angles of attack and velocities are assigned?
  • Which infinite-wing pressure channels and sample-data columns are released?
  • What uncertainty threshold defines an acceptable test condition?
  • Which sensor or setup improvements are students allowed to evaluate?

Interpretation prompt

Ask students whether a lower normalized uncertainty is always preferable. The intended answer is no: a condition also needs to be physically valid, safe, relevant to the client objective, and supported by an appropriate model.
