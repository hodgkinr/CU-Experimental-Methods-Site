E3 Week 4 (Course Week 13) · Reading

Basics of an Experimental Safety Plan

Introduces the components of a basic experimental safety plan as a professional engineering document — distinct from both the test plan and the V&V plan, but a required accompaniment to any physical experiment. Covers: hazard identification (what can go wrong, and how), risk assessment (likelihood and consequence), mitigation measures (engineering controls, administrative controls, PPE), emergency procedures, and sign-off or approval requirements. Connects to the E3 design challenge: students designing an experiment for real hardware must be able to articulate what safety considerations apply to their test setup. Placed in Week 13 alongside the test plan reading so students understand the full documentation ecosystem around a planned experiment before they finalize their V&V plan and prepare for the oral defense.

9 min Readings

Learning Objectives

  • Produce a structured V&V plan with all required components: conceptual model, formal hypothesis, system response features, validation metrics, accuracy requirements, calibration vs. validation distinction, and AMVF annotation — CLO 1, CLO 2, CLO 3
  • Apply MATLAB-based uncertainty analysis to quantify predicted measurement performance and interpret what that performance level means for the experiment's ability to inform and improve the predictive model — CLO 2, CLO 3
  • Articulate intended use as the framing constraint that connects every V&V plan component — why the model is being validated for this specific purpose, and what level of agreement is sufficient — CLO 3
  • State explicitly how the V&V plan is designed to produce data that will inform and update the predictive model — not merely test it against a binary accept/reject criterion — CLO 3

Safety and Feasibility: A Proportionate Design Check

Note the risks that could change your proposed experiment

Safety and feasibility are part of a credible experimental design. The Tier 3 proposal does not require a comprehensive safety plan or risk register. Briefly identify a hazard or practical constraint that is relevant to your proposed setup and explain a reasonable control, mitigation, or scope change.

Consider whether the proposed experiment involves moving parts, electrical power, heat, pressure, suspended loads, sharp materials, chemical exposure, lasers, or another specific hazard. Be concrete: a guarding or speed limit may matter for a rotating system; a current limit may matter for a powered circuit. Do not add generic boilerplate for hazards that do not apply.

Also identify practical constraints that could make the plan infeasible, such as equipment access, time, cost, or unavailable measurements. A narrow, realistic proposal is preferable to an ambitious plan that cannot be carried out.

If the experiment would require a detailed safety review before construction or operation, note that as a limitation. Applicable institutional or laboratory safety rules would govern any actual test.

Takeaway: Include enough safety and feasibility reasoning to show that the proposed experiment is plausible and responsibly scoped. An exhaustive safety plan is optional further engineering work, not a Tier 3 deliverable.
