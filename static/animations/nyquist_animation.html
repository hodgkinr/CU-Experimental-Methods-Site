Sampling rate, Nyquist and aliasing

The black curve is the true sine wave. The ADC only sees the silver dots, one per sample tick. The gold curve is what those samples look like: the simplest signal that passes through every dot. When the sample rate is above twice the signal frequency, gold matches black. Below that, the samples describe a different, slower signal. That is aliasing.

Frequency view. Frequencies above fs/2 fold back into the shaded band, like a mirror at the Nyquist frequency.

The rule. To capture a sine of frequency f, sample faster than 2f. Equivalently, f must be below the Nyquist frequency fs/2. Above it, the samples are identical to those of a sine at the aliased frequency |f − k·fs|, where k is the integer that makes it smallest. No processing after sampling can tell the two apart, which is why real ADCs have an analog low-pass (anti-aliasing) filter in front of them.

Exactly at Nyquist (f = fs/2) you get two samples per cycle, and the result depends on where they land. At phase 0° every sample hits a zero crossing and the signal vanishes. At 90° you catch the peaks. That is why the rule is strictly greater than 2f, and in practice engineers sample several times faster.

Display modes. "Apparent sine" draws the lowest-frequency sine through the samples. "Connect the dots" is what a naive plot of the data shows. "Sample & hold" is the staircase the ADC actually outputs, as in the quantization demo.
