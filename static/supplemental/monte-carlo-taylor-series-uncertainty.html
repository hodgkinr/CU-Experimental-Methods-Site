Taylor-Series and Monte Carlo Uncertainty Propagation

Key idea: both methods should account for the same uncertainty sources

When you report or propagate measurement uncertainty, you may have several different sources contributing to the uncertainty in a measured variable. For example, a voltage measurement might include:

  • Manufacturer accuracy from the instrument specification
  • Experimental or observation uncertainty associated with how the measurement is made
  • Standard error of the mean (SEM) associated with repeat measurements

You already account for these different sources when using the traditional Taylor-series uncertainty method. Your Monte Carlo analysis should do the same.

The important difference is how the uncertainty is propagated.

  • In the Taylor-series method, each source is first represented as a standard uncertainty, and those standard uncertainties are propagated analytically.
  • In the Monte Carlo method, each source is represented by an appropriate probability distribution, samples are drawn from those distributions, and the samples are propagated numerically through the measurement equation.

The two methods should begin with the same physical uncertainty information.

1. Review: combining uncertainty with the Taylor-series method

Suppose a measured voltage is

\[ \bar V = 10.0\ \text{V} \]

and the measurement has three independent uncertainty sources.

Manufacturer accuracy

Suppose the instrument manufacturer specifies

\[ \pm 0.10\ \text{V}. \]

If all values within that range are considered equally plausible, the uncertainty is represented by a rectangular (uniform) distribution with half-width

\[ a_{\text{mfg}} = 0.10\ \text{V}. \]

The corresponding standard uncertainty is

\[ u_{\text{mfg}} = \frac{a_{\text{mfg}}}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{0.10}{\sqrt{3}}. \]

Standard error of the mean

Suppose repeated measurements give

\[ u_{\text{SEM}} = 0.03\ \text{V}. \]

This is already expressed as a standard uncertainty, so no rectangular-distribution conversion is needed.

Observation uncertainty

Suppose the experimenter estimates an observation uncertainty of

\[ \pm 0.05\ \text{V}. \]

If this is also treated as a rectangular distribution,

\[ u_{\text{obs}} = \frac{0.05}{\sqrt{3}}. \]

Combined standard uncertainty

For independent sources, the combined standard uncertainty is

\[ u_c(V) = \sqrt{ u_{\text{mfg}}^2 + u_{\text{SEM}}^2 + u_{\text{obs}}^2 }. \]

For these values,

\[ u_c(V) \approx 0.071\ \text{V}. \]

This quantity is the combined standard uncertainty.

Important: \(u_c\) is NOT the uniform-distribution half-width \(a\)

This is one of the most important distinctions in this topic.

For a rectangular distribution,

\[ u = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}, \]

where

  • \(a\) is the half-width or bound of the rectangular distribution, and
  • \(u\) is the standard uncertainty (the standard deviation of that distribution).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{u_c \neq a} \]

in general.

The combined uncertainty \(u_c\) should not be interpreted as saying that the true value is uniformly distributed between

\[ \bar V-u_c \quad\text{and}\quad \bar V+u_c. \]

Doing that would incorrectly treat a standard uncertainty as if it were a hard rectangular bound.

This is why a Monte Carlo simulation should not normally be constructed by simply generating

\[ V \sim U(\bar V-u_c,\bar V+u_c). \]

That distribution would have a standard deviation of

\[ \frac{u_c}{\sqrt{3}}, \]

not \(u_c\), and would therefore represent a different amount of uncertainty.

2. The preferred Monte Carlo approach

The most appropriate Monte Carlo approach is to go back to the individual uncertainty sources.

For this example, the three sources can be modeled as

\[ \epsilon_{\text{mfg}} \sim U(-0.10,\,+0.10), \]

\[ \epsilon_{\text{SEM}} \sim N(0,\,0.03), \]

and

\[ \epsilon_{\text{obs}} \sim U(-0.05,\,+0.05). \]

For each Monte Carlo trial, MATLAB draws one random sample from each source and constructs a simulated voltage:

\[ V_i = \bar V + \epsilon_{\text{mfg},i} + \epsilon_{\text{SEM},i} + \epsilon_{\text{obs},i}. \]

This process is repeated many times, for example

\[ N=100{,}000. \]

The resulting collection

\[ V_1,V_2,\ldots,V_N \]

forms the Monte Carlo distribution of the measured voltage.

The standard deviation of this simulated distribution should be approximately

\[ \sigma_{\text{MC}} \approx u_c. \]

For this linear, additive example, the Taylor-series and Monte Carlo approaches should therefore give nearly the same combined standard uncertainty.

Because several independent uncertainty sources are being added together, the final Monte Carlo distribution is approximately normal even though two of the individual uncertainty sources are rectangular.

Taylor-series vs. Monte Carlo: the main distinction

A useful way to think about the two approaches is:

Taylor-series propagation

  1. Identify each uncertainty source.
  2. Convert each source to a standard uncertainty \(u_i\).
  3. Propagate the uncertainties mathematically.
  4. Report the resulting combined standard uncertainty \(u_c\).

For independent additive uncertainties,

\[ u_c=\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+\cdots}. \]

Monte Carlo propagation

  1. Identify the same uncertainty sources.
  2. Assign an appropriate probability distribution to each source.
  3. Randomly sample each distribution many times.
  4. Propagate each set of samples through the measurement equation.
  5. Examine the resulting output distribution.

The Monte Carlo method therefore retains more information about the shape of each uncertainty source.

Why use Monte Carlo if the answers are nearly identical here?

For this simple example,

\[ V = \bar V + \epsilon_1+ \epsilon_2+ \epsilon_3, \]

the measurement equation is linear and the uncertainty sources are independent. The Taylor-series method works extremely well, so Monte Carlo and analytical propagation should agree.

Monte Carlo becomes especially useful when:

  • the measurement equation is nonlinear,
  • uncertainty sources have unusual or strongly non-normal distributions,
  • the resulting output uncertainty is asymmetric,
  • variables are constrained by physical limits,
  • or the first-order Taylor approximation is no longer adequate.

In those cases, Monte Carlo can reveal features of the uncertainty distribution that a single value of \(u_c\) cannot.

Bottom line

\[ \boxed{\text{Do not create a Monte Carlo distribution from } \bar V\pm u_c \text{ as though }u_c\text{ were a rectangular half-width.}} \]

Instead,

\[ \boxed{\text{sample from the individual uncertainty-source distributions and combine them.}} \]

The Taylor-series method and Monte Carlo method are two different ways of propagating the same underlying uncertainty information.

The provided MATLAB script demonstrates this directly: the analytical calculation combines the source standard uncertainties in quadrature, while the Monte Carlo calculation samples the corresponding source distributions and propagates those samples numerically.
